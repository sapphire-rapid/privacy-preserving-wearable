Wearable Health Mesh

Health monitoring that never leaves the building.

Wristbands measure heart rate and carry a fall detector small enough to run on the band itself. Readings travel from one wearer's band to the next until they reach a gateway at the nurse station. Nothing goes to the cloud, and no raw health data leaves the site.

Who it's for

Elderly-care homes

A resident who falls in their room at night raises an alert without pressing anything. The alert reaches the nurse station through other residents' bands.

Autism hostels

Monitoring works without a phone or an app to manage, and nothing about a resident's health is sent outside the building.

Off-grid industrial sites

Where there is no Wi-Fi or mobile coverage, workers' bands pass alerts along to the site office.

One band, three parts

Each band is three off-the-shelf parts stacked in a 3D-printed case on a standard watch strap. The pulse sensor sits underneath, against the skin, and the radio sits on top, where the body blocks the least signal.

Dashed lines mark the sensor window on the underside, as in an engineering drawing.

How a reading reaches the nurse station

  1. Measure

    The band finds each heartbeat in the pulse signal and keeps a running heart rate.

  2. Pack

    Every 3 seconds it sends 8 bytes: a sequence number, the heart rate, and its RPL rank, which says how many hops it is from the gateway.

  3. Hop

    The packet goes to the band's parent, the neighbour that RPL picked as its best way out. That neighbour may be another resident's band. If the parent walks away or switches off, the band picks a new one.

  4. Hand over

    The gateway, an nRF52840 USB dongle at the root of the network, passes the packet over USB to a Raspberry Pi 4.

  5. Confirm

    The Pi logs the reading and sends an acknowledgement back down the same path, so every band knows its own round-trip time and packet loss.

    [TX] seq=0 bpm=76 rank=768 hops=2 | loss=0%
[ACK] seq=0 rtt=38ms

Fall detection sized for the band

Every 2 seconds the band condenses its accelerometer data into 9 numbers: how hard the impact was, how sudden, how long the wrist was nearly weightless, and how much its tilt changed. A logistic-regression model, nine weights and a bias, decides whether that looked like a fall, so only the result has to travel over the network. The detector is written in plain C and matches the Python reference exactly on the host; running it on live wrist data is the next step.

The model was trained and tested on SisFall, about 4,500 recorded falls and everyday activities from 38 people aged 19 to 75. Move the threshold to see the trade-off it has to make.

0.93

The final design adds a 3-second stillness check after any trigger, because someone who has fallen usually stays down. With it, the band catches 94.9% of falls and raises a false alarm on 9.8% of everyday-activity trials. This was measured with subject-wise nested cross-validation, so no test subject's data was used to tune it.

How far one hop reaches

Two single-hop campaigns at the radio's maximum of +8 dBm, with the small chip antennas the boards ship with: close range in line of sight with the band tethered to a laptop, and an outdoor open-field test. Loss rises with distance rather than cutting out, which is why the mesh relays through nearer bands instead of relying on one long link.

Where the project stands

Working now

  • Readings travel from band to gateway and acknowledgements come back, with every packet carrying the band's RPL rank
  • A heart-rate driver for the MAX30102, written for RIOT OS
  • Single-hop link measured out to about 55 m outdoors
  • A fall model validated on SisFall and exported to C for the band
  • Gateway firmware that configures itself on boot

Next

  • Alert-priority routing: only a band with an active alert changes how the network routes
  • A heart-rate check after a fall, to grade the alert as critical
  • Fall detection tested on the band itself, with our own recordings
  • Multi-band trials on site, compared against commercial wearables
